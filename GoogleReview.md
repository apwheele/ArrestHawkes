## Summary
This paper introduces a methodological extension to spatio-temporal self-exciting point process (Hawkes) models of crime to quantify how police arrests interrupt ongoing chains of near-repeat offenses. By multiplying a crime's remaining triggering kernel by a dampening factor $\kappa_i \ge 0$ at the exact timestamp $a_i$ when its linked arrest occurs, the model avoids the immortal time bias of treating an eventual arrest as a fixed baseline trait while preserving a non-negative conditional intensity and a closed-form branching cluster structure. The author validates the estimator across six simulation scenarios—including offender sprees that violate the Poisson offspring assumption—and applies it to open municipal crime and arrest records for car prowls in Seattle (2019–2026) and shootings in Chicago (2016–2026), translating the fitted parameters into expected counts of direct and multi-generational follow-on crimes prevented as a function of time-to-arrest.

## Strengths
* **Methodological Novelty and Elimination of Immortal Time Bias:** Introducing the arrest dampening multiplier dynamically at the arrest timestamp $a_i$ ($\kappa_i^{A_i(t)}$) rather than as a time-invariant mark at the offense time $t_i$ eliminates immortal time bias without requiring the nonlinear link functions of general inhibitory Hawkes processes.
* **Direct Policy Quantification via Closed-Form Branching Estimators:** Preserving the linear immigrant-offspring branching structure yields closed-form expressions for the log-likelihood compensator, the expected count of prevented direct and descendant crimes, and the continuous decay curve of an arrest's preventive value with delay $d_i = a_i - t_i$.
* **Rigorous Simulation Benchmarking Under Misspecification:** The simulation study thoroughly evaluates both well-specified Hawkes worlds and misspecified sequential offender spree processes, demonstrating that the dynamic timestamp formulation accurately recovers $\kappa$ and the count of prevented crimes while fixed-mark specifications miss 40% to over 90% of prevented offenses.
* **Careful Empirical Validation and Sober Policy Accounting:** The author systematically audits open crime and arrest data across major U.S. cities, cross-validates linked Chicago shooting arrest shares against FOIA-reported clearance rates, and provides a realistic accounting of scale showing why rapid on-scene arrests cut car prowl chains by 95% ($\kappa = 0.05$) yet account for a small fraction (0.06%) of total offenses due to short chain lengths and low arrest rates.

## Weaknesses
* **Pre-Computed Unweighted Spatial Background Density:** The spatial background density $b(s)$ is constructed via variable-bandwidth kernel density estimation weighting all observed crimes equally rather than iteratively weighting events by their background probability via stochastic declustering, which can allow tight clusters of triggered crimes to inflate $b(s)$ at small nearest-neighbor bandwidths ($k=5$) and condition standard errors on a fixed plug-in surface (Reference Paper: Self-Exciting Point Process Modeling of Crime, George O. Mohler, Martin B. Short, P. Jeffrey Brantingham, Frederic P. Schoenberg, and George E. Tita, 2011).
* **Inconsistency in Model Selection Reporting Between Main Text and Appendix:** Please consider reconciling the model selection rule in Section 5 and Appendix Table 7 for Chicago shootings, where Table 7 marks the Power Law kernel ($k=5$, log-likelihood $-495,927.2$) with an asterisk as having the highest log-likelihood, whereas Tables 4–6 and the main text report the more parsimonious Exponential kernel ($k=5$, log-likelihood $-495,927.3$) that is favored by AIC.
* **Homogeneous Branching and Variance Approximations in Prevented Crime Counts:** Scaling removed direct offspring $D$ by $1/(1-n_0)$ and computing prediction intervals via normal quadrature with homogeneous Borel–Tanner tree variance $n_0/(1-n_0)^3$ relies on implicit assumptions that counterfactual descendants share the unweighted sample-average productivity $n_0$ across generations and does not propagate estimation uncertainty in $\hat{\theta}$ and $\hat{S}$ when $\hat{\kappa}$ is near the zero boundary.
* **Additive Kernel Tension Under Multi-Crime Spree Clearances and Tied Timestamps:** Because the Hawkes process assigns independent, additive kernels to each crime in a spree and uses strict time ordering ($t_i < t$), clearing multiple prior offenses with a single arrest overstates prevented crimes by summing residual kernels across the series, while exact timestamp ties ($17\%$ of Seattle car prowls) and overnight reporting windows conservatively exclude co-occurring spree offenses from mutual excitation.

### [Framing, Introduction, Policy Discussion, and Synthesis] (Pages: 1-2, 13-15)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Reconciling the discussion of multi-crime offender clearances with the spree simulation results (Pages 13–14 vs. Page 6):**
   In Section 6, the limitations note that *"The arrest link is to one report, and an arrest that should be credited against a string of an offender's crimes is credited against one"* (page 13), and the extensions propose that *"With offender identifiers, arrests could dampen the kernels of all of a person's linked crimes at once"* (page 14). As a potential concern, this framing appears to be in slight tension with the simulation analysis in Section 3 (*"Sprees, earlier crimes cleared"*, Table 1 and page 6), which directly tests the effect of having a single arrest dampen 70% of an offender's earlier crimes at the arrest time $a_i$. Because the Hawkes model approximates a single-offender spree by assigning an additive, independent triggering kernel $\theta_i g(t - t_i) h(s - s_i)$ to every crime in the series, dampening all of an offender's linked crimes simultaneously sums the removed residual kernels across multiple offenses from the same spree. In the simulation, this causes the model to overstate prevented crimes by 15% (542 estimated vs. 472 true) and prompts the conclusion on page 6 that *"Where an arrest clears a string of crimes it should be attached to the last of them."* Conversely, attaching the arrest only to the last crime (*"Sprees"*) leaves the residual kernel tails of earlier offenses in the series undampened, underestimating prevented crimes by 12% (414 vs. 469). Clarifying this structural trade-off in Section 6 would strengthen the discussion: when offender identifiers are available, accurately crediting an arrest across a string of linked offenses requires not only linking the arrest to prior offenses in the series, but also accounting for the fact that offenses committed by the same person do not generate independent, additive offspring trees once the offender is incapacitated.

2. **Completeness of the Confounding Bias Direction in the Discussion (Page 13 vs. Page 5):**
   In Section 6 (page 13, paragraph 5), the text notes: *"The estimate of $\kappa$ is observational. Arrested crimes are not a random sample, and I have only crude covariates to adjust for the difference; the simulation shows the direction of the bias when a trait raises both productivity and the chance of arrest."* While this accurately summarizes the specific *"Confounded"* simulation scenario in Section 3 (where positive correlation between baseline productivity $\theta_i$ and arrest probability biases $\kappa$ upward toward 1 and understates crimes prevented), Section 2.7 (page 5) explicitly establishes that unmeasured confounding can run in either direction depending on the offense mechanism: *"A domestic shooting is both easier to clear and less likely to spark retaliation, which would make arrests look more effective than they are; a prolific offender who is eventually caught would make them look less effective."* Briefly reiterating in Section 6 that unmeasured confounding can bias $\kappa$ either upward or downward—depending on whether cleared crimes have higher or lower baseline productivity—would prevent readers from concluding that observational confounding in this framework only makes estimates conservative.

3. **Methodological Considerations for the Time-Only NIBRS Extension (Page 14):**
   In Section 6 (page 14, paragraph 2), the paper proposes a time-only version of the model for National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS) data, noting: *"A model in time alone would also count retaliation wherever in the city it happens, at the cost of having to pick it out from every other shooting."* As a potential methodological consideration, removing the spatial kernel $h(s - s_i)$ in a purely temporal Hawkes process at the city or agency level substantially lowers the signal-to-noise ratio because every subsequent crime across the entire jurisdiction enters the risk set following an initiating crime and its arrest. Furthermore, separating true citywide contagion from unmodeled short-term temporal clustering (such as weekend evening spikes, weather shocks, or holidays not absorbed by a quarterly step function $\nu(t)$) becomes much more difficult without spatial localization. Briefly noting the need for finer temporal background controls (e.g., day-of-week and time-of-day periodic components) or focusing on smaller jurisdictions when moving to a time-only NIBRS specification would add useful context to this proposed extension.

### [Statistical Model, Likelihood Derivation, and Crimes-Prevented Estimators] (Pages: 2-5)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Implicit Branching Approximations in the Total Crimes-Prevented Estimator ($P$) and Prediction Intervals (Subsection 2.6, pages 4–5):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Subsection 2.6, the direct offspring removed within the observation window $[0, T]$ are computed as
     $$D = \sum_{i: a_i \le T} \theta_i (1 - \kappa_i) [S(c_i) - S(T - t_i)],$$
     and scaled to total prevented crimes via
     $$P = \frac{B}{1 - n_0} - \frac{B}{1 - n_1} = \frac{D}{1 - n_0},$$
     where $n_0 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \theta_i$ and $n_1 = n_0 - D / N$. While this identity holds algebraically when $B$ is defined via the plug-in relation $B = N(1 - n_1) = N(1 - n_0) + D$ (which could be stated explicitly so the reader does not confuse $B$ with the unconstrained compensator integral $\int_0^T \int \mu(t, s)\,ds\,dt$), applying the homogeneous, infinite-horizon branching multiplier $1 / (1 - n_0)$ and the compound Poisson–Galton–Watson variance formula relies on four implicit approximations that warrant clarification:
     1. *Covariate heterogeneity across events and generations:* Subsection 2.1 allows productivity to depend on covariates, $\theta_i = \exp(x_i^\top \gamma)$ (such as fatal vs. non-fatal shootings in Chicago, or the high-productivity trait in the confounded simulation). Scaling $D$ by $1 / (1 - n_0)$ and using the homogeneous Borel progeny variance $n_0 / (1 - n_0)^3$ assumes that unobserved counterfactual offspring—and all of their subsequent descendants—have an expected productivity equal to the unweighted sample average $n_0$ across all (predominantly background) events, independent of the parent crime's traits. If triggered follow-on crimes have a different covariate mix than background crimes, or if offender/location covariates persist across a chain, the expected size of a descendant tree initiated by a removed offspring depends on the expected productivity of *triggered* offspring rather than the overall sample mean $n_0$, and the variance of total family size includes an additional between-covariate variance component.
     2. *Global vs. marginal counterfactual arrest regimes:* Multiplying $D$ by $1 / (1 - n_0)$ assumes that none of the prevented follow-on crimes would themselves have led to an arrest that dampened their subsequent branches. While this matches the global counterfactual stated in the text (*"had the same arrests been made with no effect on triggering"*, i.e., $\kappa = 1$ everywhere), when evaluating the marginal value of a *single* arrest via $\bar{\theta}(1 - \kappa)S(d) / (1 - n_0)$ while holding the baseline arrest process active for other crimes, any follow-on crimes prevented by that single arrest would still be subject to the baseline probability of arrest and dampening ($1 / (1 - n_1)$ rather than $1 / (1 - n_0)$). Although the numerical difference is minor here because $n_0$ and arrest rates are small, distinguishing between switching off the arrest effect globally and removing a single arrest under the active arrest regime would sharpen the causal interpretation.
     3. *Finite-window truncation vs. infinite-horizon branching:* The direct count $D$ explicitly right-censors direct offspring at the end of the observation window $T$ via $-S(T - t_i)$, whereas $n_0$, the multiplier $1 / (1 - n_0)$, and the family-size variance $n_0 / (1 - n_0)^3$ are infinite-horizon steady-state expressions. Multiplying window-truncated $D$ by $1 / (1 - n_0)$ credits each removed direct crime with its full infinite-horizon descendant tree (including secondary and higher-order offspring after $T$). Because the estimated temporal kernels decay within hours or days while $T$ spans several years, boundary truncation is negligible in practice, which could be briefly noted to justify combining finite-window $D$ with the infinite-horizon multiplier.
     4. *Prediction interval construction:* The prediction intervals combine the profile-likelihood interval for $\kappa$ (refitting the other parameters at the endpoints of $\kappa$) with the process variance in quadrature. This approach has two statistical limitations worth noting: (i) profiling over $\kappa$ alone does not propagate the sampling variance of $\hat{\theta}$ and the temporal decay parameters $\hat{S}$ at a fixed $\kappa$—most notably when $\hat{\kappa}$ is close to the boundary $\kappa_L = 0$ (as in Seattle car prowls, where $\hat{\kappa} = 0.05$ and $\kappa_L = 0.00$), where moving $\kappa$ from $\hat{\kappa}$ to $\kappa_L$ produces almost no change in $P \propto \theta(1 - \kappa)$ even though $\hat{\theta}$ and $\hat{S}$ have non-zero estimation error; and (ii) normal quadrature with a constant plug-in variance treats a discrete, non-negative, right-skewed compound Poisson–branching count distribution as symmetric, which can yield negative lower bounds when expected counts are near zero (even for $\kappa \le 1$) and ignores that the process variance scales linearly with $D(\kappa)$ across the profile limits.

2. **Specification, Weighting, and Normalization of the Leave-One-Out Spatial Background $b(s)$ (Subsections 2.4–2.5, page 4):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Subsection 2.5 specifies a separable background $\mu(t, s) = \nu(t) b(s)$, where $\nu(t)$ is a quarterly step function and $b(s)$ is a variable-bandwidth Gaussian kernel density estimate in which "the density at crime $j$ leaves out crime $j$'s own kernel," referencing Mohler et al. (2011). Two details of this construction are omitted from the text and represent potential concerns for standalone reproducibility and estimation properties:
     1. *Uniform plug-in weights vs. declustered weighting:* Unlike Mohler et al. (2011), where events in the background KDE are weighted by their estimated background probabilities via stochastic declustering (EM), the text does not state whether $b(s)$ is pre-computed once prior to optimization with uniform weights across all $N$ observed crimes. If $b(s)$ is fixed beforehand with uniform weights, then even though crime $j$'s own kernel is omitted when evaluating $b(s_j)$, other crimes belonging to the same near-repeat cluster or spree remain in the sum for $b(s_j)$. Under a small nearest-neighbor bandwidth (such as $k = 5$ with a 40-meter floor), tight spatial clusters of triggered offspring directly inflate the local background density $b(s)$, allowing $\mu(t, s)$ to absorb short-term spatial contagion and potentially biasing $\theta$ downward. Moreover, holding the plug-in surface $b(s)$ fixed when inverting the Hessian of $\ell$ conditions on $b(s)$ rather than propagating KDE estimation uncertainty into the standard errors.
     2. *Normalization across the point-event sum and the compensator integral:* In the first term of the log-likelihood $\ell = \sum_{j=1}^N \log \lambda(t_j, s_j) - \int_0^T \int \lambda(t, s)\,ds\,dt$, omitting crime $j$'s kernel leaves $N - 1$ Gaussian kernels in $b(s_j)$, whereas in the compensator integral $\int_0^T \int \mu(t, s)\,ds\,dt$, the spatial integral $\int b(s)\,ds$ is evaluated over the continuous plane. Explicitly writing out the mathematical formula for $b(s_j)$ (confirming that the leave-one-out sum is divided by $N - 1$ so that $\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} b_{-j}(s)\,ds = 1$) and stating that $\int b(s)\,ds = 1$ so that the background compensator reduces to $\int_0^T \nu(t)\,dt$ would make the likelihood equations fully reproducible from the text alone.

3. **Sequential Exogeneity in the Partial Likelihood and Retrospective Multi-Crime Clearances in $D$ (Subsections 2.4, 2.6, and 2.7, pages 4–5):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Subsection 2.4 treats arrest times $a_i$ as given in a partial likelihood framework (*"I do not model when arrests happen, only what happens to the crime rate after they do"*), and Subsection 2.7 states three identification conditions, including that "the arrest must not have been caused by the absence of follow-on crimes or the reverse, beyond the time ordering the model already respects" and that the linked arrest must be the relevant one. Formally, factoring the arrest timing distribution out of the full point-process likelihood requires sequential exogeneity (non-informative intervention timing) in continuous time: the conditional hazard of an arrest $a_i$ at time $t > t_i$ must not depend on unobserved factors governing future crime intensity given the history $\mathcal{H}_{t^-}$, nor be retrospectively determined by the arrival of follow-on crimes in $(t_i, t)$.
   - In policing—and explicitly in the scenario discussed in Subsection 2.3 (page 4, *"If a car prowler is caught on the third night and the arrest clears all three reports..."*) and simulated in Section 3 (*"Sprees, earlier crimes cleared"*)—an offender is frequently arrested precisely because they commit a follow-on crime at time $t_2 > t_1$ and are apprehended at the scene of $t_2$, which retrospectively clears the earlier crime $t_1$ with arrest timestamp $a_1 = a_2 = t_2$. Here, $a_1$ is a stopping time directly triggered by the arrival of its own offspring event at $t_2$. While entering the arrest at $a_i$ avoids immortal time bias prior to $a_i$, retrospectively linking a single arrest timestamp $a$ to $m$ prior offenses in the same chain causes the additive estimator $D = \sum_{i: a_i \le T} \theta_i (1 - \kappa_i)[S(c_i) - S(T - t_i)]$ in Subsection 2.6 to credit the arrest with independently removing the remaining triggering kernels of all $m$ crimes, even though the offender represents a single continuation risk. Explicitly connecting the partial likelihood's exogeneity requirement and the third condition in Subsection 2.7 to the summation in $D$ would explain mathematically why multi-crime clearances inflate total prevented crimes in the simulation results even when $\kappa$ itself is consistently estimated near zero.

4. **Domain Restrictions on $a_i \ge t_i$ and Tied Timestamps in the Formal Model Specification (Subsections 2.1, 2.2, and 2.4, pages 2–4):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Subsections 2.1 and 2.2, the sums in $\lambda(t, s)$ run over strict inequalities $i : t_i < t$ and $i : a_i < t$, and Subsection 2.2 rewrites the conditional intensity as a two-sum linear regression:
     $$\lambda(t, s) = \mu(t, s) + \beta_1 \sum_{i: t_i < t} g(t - t_i) h(s - s_i) + \beta_2 \sum_{i: a_i < t} g(t - t_i) h(s - s_i).$$
     For the second sum (indexed by $\{i: a_i < t\}$) to be algebraically identical to the dampened portion of the first sum (indexed by $\{i: t_i < t\}$ with indicator $A_i(t) = \mathbf{1}(a_i < t)$), and for $d_i = a_i - t_i$ (Subsection 2.2) and $c_i = \min(a_i, T) - t_i$ (Subsection 2.4) to remain non-negative inside the survival function $S(u) = \int_u^\infty g(v)\,dv$ (defined on $u \ge 0$), it is required that $a_i \ge t_i$ for all $i$. While later empirical sections mention that a small number of arrests logged before the reported offense time are recoded to $a_i = t_i$ (Section 4, page 8) and that offenses sharing identical timestamps ($t_i = t_j$) cannot trigger one another because of the strict inequality $t_i < t$ (Section 5.4, page 13), stating the domain constraint $a_i \ge t_i$ (or defining $c_i = \max(0, \min(a_i, T) - t_i)$) and noting the consequence of strict time ordering directly in Section 2 would make the mathematical exposition self-contained.

### [Simulation Study and Model Misspecification Analysis] (Pages: 5-7)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Statistical Mechanism Behind the Spree Estimation Bias and Prediction Interval Under-Coverage (Pages 5–7):**
   - **Mechanism of the 12% underestimation in the baseline *Sprees* scenario (414 estimated vs. 469 true prevented):** This is a potential concern regarding the completeness of the statistical explanation on page 6. The text attributes the approximation error in the spree worlds to the difference between Bernoulli and Poisson offspring distributions ("In the spree worlds the model is only an approximation: an offender commits at most one next crime, where the model's crimes have a Poisson number of offspring"). However, matching the first moment of a Bernoulli($p$) continuation process and a Poisson($\theta$) branching process yields the exact same expected total cluster size $1/(1-p)$ when $p = \theta$, so replacing Poisson offspring with Bernoulli continuation does not by itself induce a downward bias in $P = D/(1 - n_0)$. A more direct structural reason the point-process estimator underestimates prevented crimes by 12% in the *Sprees* scenario is the mismatch between single-crime arrest linkage in an additive Hawkes process and sequential offender continuation under delayed arrests:
     - First, in the simulated spree process (page 5), half of arrests occur after an exponential delay averaging two days, which matches the mean gap to the offender's next crime ($p = 0.45$, mean gap two days). Consequently, an offender who is not arrested at the scene of crime $i$ will commit crime $i+1$ *before* the delayed arrest triggered by crime $i$ takes place ($t_{i+1} < a_i$) in roughly $0.45 \times 0.5 = 22.5\%$ of delayed-arrest cases. In the true spree process, that delayed arrest at $a_i$ still apprehends the offender and terminates the chain after crime $i+1$ (preventing crime $i+2$ and beyond). In the fitted Hawkes model, however—where the arrest is attached only to the initiating crime $i$—crime $i$'s arrest at $a_i$ only dampens the older, further-decayed tail $S(a_i - t_i)$ of crime $i$'s own kernel while leaving the more recent crime $i+1$'s kernel $g(t - t_{i+1})$ untouched at full strength. Moreover, because crime $i+1$ has its subsequent spree cut short by crime $i$'s delayed arrest without having an arrest recorded on crime $i+1$ itself, it enters the likelihood as an unarrested crime with truncated follow-on activity, pulling down the estimated baseline productivity $\hat{\theta}$.
     - Second, in a sequential spree ($t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_m$), only the most recent crime $t_m$ generates the next offense, whereas the additive Hawkes model attributes triggering intensity simultaneously to all prior crimes in the spree. When an arrest occurs on the latest crime $t_m$, dampening only crime $t_m$'s kernel in the model does not remove the remaining tails attributed to earlier unarrested crimes ($t_1, \dots, t_{m-1}$) in the same spree.
     - Explicitly noting these mechanisms would clarify why linking an arrest only to the initiating crime $i$ understates prevented crimes in simple sprees, and would also refine the linkage recommendation on page 6 ("Where an arrest clears a string of crimes it should be attached to the last of them") to specifically recommend attaching a spree-ending arrest to the *most recent crime prior to the arrest* ($t_j \le a_i$).
   - **Cause of prediction interval under-coverage in the spree scenarios (69% and 62% coverage):** At the bottom of page 6 and top of page 7, the text states that in both spree scenarios "the intervals, which take the model as given, are too narrow: they cover the true count in 69% and 62% of worlds." This is a potential concern because describing the intervals simply as "too narrow" may suggest that the Poisson branching variance formula in Section 2.6 ($D[n_0/(1-n_0)^3 + 1/(1-n_0)^2]$) underestimates the stochastic variability of the spree process. In fact, a Bernoulli($p$) offspring distribution has strictly *smaller* conditional variance ($p(1-p) < p$, leading to geometric spree size variance $p/(1-p)^2$) than a Poisson($p$) branching process (which has family size variance $p/(1-p)^3$). Thus, the Hawkes process variance formula in Section 2.6 actually overstates the true random variability of the realized spree count around its expectation (yielding a 95% half-width of roughly $68\text{--}75$ crimes, which adequately spans the stochastic spread of the 100 realizations around their center in Figure 2). As shown in Table 1 and Figure 2, the drop in coverage from 95% to 69% and 62% is primarily driven by the **centering bias (location shift)** of the point estimator under model misspecification ($\approx -55$ crimes in *Sprees* and $+70$ crimes in *Sprees, earlier crimes cleared*, shifting the center of the prediction intervals by roughly $1.5\sigma$ to $1.8\sigma$ away from zero in Figure 2) rather than underestimated process variance. Explicitly distinguishing between centering bias in $\hat{P}$ and interval width would make this diagnostic interpretation more precise.

2. **Impact of Timestamp Discretization, Exact Ties ($t_i = t_j$), and Retroactive Overnight Reporting Windows on Seattle Car Prowl Estimates (Sections 4 and 5.4)**
   - Section 4 (page 8) notes that the recorded offense time for a Seattle car prowl is the start of the reporting window provided by the victim (which can be off by hours for overnight thefts), and that 34 car prowl arrests logged shortly before the offense time were truncated to scene arrests ($d_i = 0$). Section 5.4 (page 13) further observes that 49% of car prowl offense times are recorded on the hour and 17% share an exact timestamp with the preceding event, noting that *"Crimes with identical recorded times also cannot be ordered, so neither counts as triggering the other, which makes the estimate of $\theta$ conservative."*
   - While the text acknowledges the conservative effect on $\theta$, this temporal discretization introduces two additional methodological concerns that warrant discussion or sensitivity analysis:
     1. *Asymmetric treatment of spatial versus temporal ties during within-night sprees:* To prevent identical block-level coordinates from driving $\sigma \to 0$, Gaussian noise with a 30-meter standard deviation is added to coordinates (page 8). However, temporal ties are left unperturbed under the strict inequality $t_i < t$ in the conditional intensity $\lambda(t, s)$. When an offender breaks into multiple cars along a street or in a parking garage during the same night and victims report a standard start-of-window hour (e.g., 10:00 PM or midnight, or morning discovery times), those co-located incidents frequently share identical timestamps ($t_i = t_j$) and are excluded from mutual excitation right where the power-law kernel peaks (median lag of 4.0 hours, Table 5). Testing whether adding small temporal jitter to break exact or on-the-hour ties alters $\hat{\theta}$ and the count of prevented crimes would help bound the magnitude of this downward bias.
     2. *Differential timestamp precision between arrested and unarrested offenses:* Because 75% of car prowl arrests occur within an hour of the recorded offense ("people caught in the act," page 8), crimes with an on-scene arrest are typically logged with a precise police encounter timestamp, whereas unarrested overnight car prowls reflect victim-reported window start times. Conversely, if an offender breaks into a car at 3:00 AM and is arrested on the scene, while the victim reports the offense window as starting at 10:00 PM the evening before, the measured arrest delay $c_i = a_i - t_i$ is artificially inflated to 5 hours, shifting an on-scene arrest into the tail of the kernel and understating the removed direct offspring $D = \sum \theta_i(1-\kappa_i)[S(c_i) - S(T-t_i)]$. Similarly, if an earlier unarrested break-in from the same spree is given a next-morning discovery timestamp ($t_j > a_i$), it will appear in the data as occurring *after* the arrest. Discussing how this differential measurement error affects $\hat{\kappa}$, $D$, and the early lag bins in Figure 3 would strengthen the empirical validation.

3. **Unstated Administrative Linkage Structure for Multi-Offense Sprees and Multi-Arrest Incidents (Sections 4 and 5.2)**
   - *Single arrest clearing multiple reports:* In the simulation study (Section 3, page 6), the *"Sprees, earlier crimes cleared"* scenario demonstrates that when a single arrest clears multiple earlier crimes in an offender's spree and is recorded with the same arrest timestamp across those reports, the model overstates prevented crimes by 15% (Table 1); the text concludes that *"Where an arrest clears a string of crimes it should be attached to the last of them."* In Section 4 (pages 7–8), each shooting and car prowl is linked to the earliest arrest sharing its case/report number. While Section 6 (page 13) notes that *"The arrest link is to one report, and an arrest that should be credited against a string of an offender's crimes is credited against one,"* Section 4 does not explicitly state whether the Seattle or Chicago open arrest tables ever link the same arrest event (e.g., via an arrest ID or identical arrest timestamp and block when an offender is caught after breaking into multiple vehicles, each assigned a separate victim report number) to more than one incident report, or if each administrative arrest record links strictly to a single report number.
   - *Ordering of multiple arrests per case number under charge filtering:* Section 4 (page 7) explains that each Chicago shooting is linked to the *earliest* arrest under its case number, and notes that *"of the linked shootings, 15% have only arrests on gun possession, drug, or other charges, which may be of a victim or bystander."* In Section 5.2 (page 11), a sensitivity analysis counting *"only arrests with a violent charge or on a warrant"* shifts the estimate of $\kappa$ from $0.83$ ($p = 0.683$) to $0.45$ ($p = 0.255$). In a shooting incident, responding officers may immediately arrest a victim or bystander at the scene on a weapon-possession or drug charge ($a_i \approx t_i$), while the shooter is arrested days or weeks later under the same case number on a violent charge. Clarifying whether the violent-charge sensitivity analysis links each shooting to the *earliest violent/warrant arrest* under that case number (rather than filtering the already-linked earliest overall arrest), and how many incidents have multiple arrests across different dates or charge types, would improve the transparency of the linkage pipeline.

4. **Immortal Time Grouping in the First Bin (`0-6 h`) of the Descriptive Follow-On Rate Plot (Figure 3, Page 9)**
   - Figure 3 plots the empirical rate of follow-on crimes within 200 meters across time-lag bins (`0-6 h`, `6 h-1 d`, `1-3 d`, etc.), comparing earlier crimes with `"No arrest"` (gray) against earlier crimes with `"Arrest within a day"` (blue, $a_i - t_i \le 24\text{ hours}$).
   - Because the blue group conditions on an arrest occurring anywhere within the first 24 hours, the first bin (`0-6 h`) includes follow-on crimes that occurred *before* the arrest for any case where the arrest happened between 6 and 24 hours after the offense. This reintroduces the fixed-mark (immortal time) effect discussed in Section 2.3 into the `0-6 h` descriptive bin. While 75% of Seattle car prowl arrests occur within an hour (making the distortion minimal for car prowls), a substantial share of day-one Chicago shooting arrests occur between 1 and 24 hours, which helps explain why the empirical blue curve for Chicago shootings starts high at `0-6 h` ($\approx 0.0136$, well above the dashed model-implied curve) before dropping sharply in the `6 h-1 d` bin ($\approx 0.0022$). Adding a brief note in the text or caption of Figure 3 clarifying that the `"Arrest within a day"` group includes some pre-arrest exposure in the `0-6 h` bin would prevent readers from interpreting the `0-6 h` point in the left panel as a failure of the model fit.

5. **Sensitivity of the Chicago Shooting $\hat{\kappa}$ Estimate to Random Spatial Jittering (Section 5.2, Page 11)**
   - To resolve block-level coordinate masking, Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 30 meters is added to each coordinate (Section 4, page 8). Section 5.2 reports $\hat{\kappa} = 0.83$ for the main coordinate draw in Chicago shootings, but notes that repeating the fit with two alternative random draws of the 30-meter Gaussian coordinate noise yields $\hat{\kappa} = 0.63$ and $0.62$.
   - While all three estimates have wide confidence intervals overlapping $1.0$, a shift from $0.83$ to $0.62$ across 30-meter spatial noise realizations is notable given that the estimated triggering spatial scale is $\hat{\sigma} = 610$ meters (more than 20 times the jittering standard deviation), where a 30-meter perturbation has a negligible direct effect on the triggering kernel $h(s - s_i)$. This sensitivity likely operates through the variable-bandwidth background density $b(s)$ at $k = 5$ nearest neighbors (which has a 40-meter floor, right at the scale of the 30-meter noise): when multiple shootings occur on the same block, their jittered pairwise distances directly alter the local leave-one-out background density $b(s_j)$, shifting how much of same-block repeat clustering is absorbed by $\mu(t, s)$ versus credited to self-excitation. Briefly explaining this mechanism in Section 5.2 or 5.4 would clarify why 30-meter coordinate perturbations move $\hat{\kappa}$ by $0.20$ when $\hat{\sigma} = 610$ meters.
